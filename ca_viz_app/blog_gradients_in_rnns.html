CA_RNN — training notes

Gradients through time

what loss.backward() is actually doing inside a recurrent model, and why the loss printed 0.0000 until it didn't

I recently caught a bug in my training loop: an epoch's average loss was computed by dividing by the number of samples instead of the number of batches, quietly shrinking a perfectly normal loss value into something that rounded to zero at four decimal places. Chasing it down meant going back to first principles — what a gradient actually is, and what backpropagation is doing to produce the numbers my training loop logs every epoch. These are my notes, worked out against Chapter 2 and Chapter 13 of Simone Scardapane's Alice's Adventures in a Differentiable Wonderland, and grounded in the VanillaRNN, GRU, and LSTM models I'm actually training.

1. Derivative → gradient → Jacobian

A derivative is defined for a scalar function of a scalar input, \(f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\). Once the input becomes a vector \(\mathbf{x} \sim (d)\), you have to pick a direction to perturb it in. Perturbing along one axis at a time gives a partial derivative; stacking all \(d\) of them gives the gradient:

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \partial f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \partial_{x_1} f(\mathbf{x}) \\ \vdots \\ \partial_{x_d} f(\mathbf{x}) \end{bmatrix} \] Def. D.2.6

That's the case for a scalar output. If the output is also a vector \(\mathbf{y} \sim (o)\), you need one gradient per output component, stacked into an \((o, d)\) matrix called the Jacobian:

\[ \partial f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial y_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial y_1}{\partial x_d} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial y_o}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial y_o}{\partial x_d} \end{pmatrix} \sim (o, d) \] Def. D.2.7

A gradient is just the Jacobian's special case when \(o = 1\). The reason this matters for training: composing functions composes their Jacobians by matrix multiplication,

\[ \partial [f(g(\mathbf{x}))] = [\partial f(\bullet)]\,\partial g(\mathbf{x}) \] E.2.23

A neural network is nothing but a long composition of functions (matmuls, nonlinearities, recurrent steps), so its loss's gradient with respect to any parameter is, in principle, a long chain of Jacobian multiplications. In practice nobody ever materializes those matrices.

2. Reverse-mode autodiff: what .backward() does

Because the final output of a network is a single scalar (the loss), the leftmost term in the full chain is a row vector, not a matrix. That means the whole chain can be computed right-to-left as a sequence of cheap vector–matrix products instead of ever forming a full Jacobian:

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf{w}_i} y = \mathbf{1}^\top \big[\partial_{\mathbf{h}_{l-1}} \mathbf{h}_l\big] \cdots \big[\partial_{\mathbf{h}_i} \mathbf{h}_{i+1}\big] \big[\partial_{\mathbf{w}_i} \mathbf{h}_i\big] \] E.6.2

Working right-to-left, each step updates a running row vector — the adjoint — by multiplying it with the next local Jacobian:

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf{h}} \leftarrow \widetilde{\mathbf{h}}\big[\partial_{\mathbf{h}_{i-1}} \mathbf{h}_i\big] \] E.6.3

This is exactly reverse-mode automatic differentiation, a.k.a. backpropagation: run the forward pass once, storing intermediate activations, then walk backward multiplying adjoints by local Jacobians until you reach a leaf parameter. That's what happens, layer by layer, inside every loss.backward() call.

3. A recurrent layer, concretely

Strip a recurrent layer down to its two functions — a state transition \(f\) and a readout \(g\):

\[ \mathbf{s}_i = f(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \mathbf{x}_i), \qquad \mathbf{h}_i = g(\mathbf{s}_i, \mathbf{x}_i) \] Def. D.13.1

The classic ("vanilla"/Elman) recurrent layer instantiates both as linear maps followed by a nonlinearity:

\[ f(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \mathbf{x}_i) = \phi(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad g(\mathbf{s}_i, \mathbf{x}_i) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{s}_i + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{x}_i \] E.13.14–15

This is precisely what nn.RNN wraps in the project's model:

src/models/VanillaRNN.py 
class VanillaRNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_dim, hidden_dim, output_dim, num_layers=1):
        super().__init__()
        self.rnn = nn.RNN(input_dim, hidden_dim, num_layers, batch_first=True)
        self.fc = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, output_dim)

    def forward(self, x):
        out, _ = self.rnn(x)
        out = self.fc(out[:, -1, :])
        return out

self.rnn is \(f\) applied once per timestep in the sequence; self.fc applied to the final hidden state is \(g\), evaluated only at the last \(i\) since that's all this project's forecasting task needs.

4. Backprop through time, and why gradients explode or vanish

Unroll the transition function over a sequence of length \(n\) and ask: how does the final state \(\mathbf{s}_n\) depend on the shared weight matrix \(\mathbf{A}\)? Because \(\mathbf{A}\) is reused at every step, its total gradient is a sum over every timestep's contribution, each one weighted by a cumulative product of Jacobians reaching back from the end of the sequence:

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_i = \prod_{j=i+1}^{n} \partial_{\mathbf{s}_{j-1}} f(\mathbf{s}_{j-1}, \mathbf{x}_j) \] E.13.16
\[ \partial_{\mathbf{A}} \mathbf{s}_n = \partial_{\mathbf{A}} f(\mathbf{s}_{n-1}, \mathbf{x}_n) + \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_i \big[\partial_{\mathbf{A}} f(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \mathbf{x}_i)\big] \] E.13.17

Written this way, reverse-mode autodiff over a recurrent layer is called backpropagation through time (BPTT). It's the same adjoint recursion from Section 2, just walking backward one timestep at a time instead of one network layer at a time.

why this matters in practice

Each factor in the cumulative product \(\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_i\) involves the derivative of the activation function, multiplied repeatedly. If those factors are consistently <1, the product shrinks toward zero over long sequences (vanishing gradients); if consistently >1, it blows up (exploding gradients). This is exactly what the per-parameter gradient norms in my training loop are meant to catch.

This is the block that logs it, right after loss.backward():

src/utils/train_loop.py 
with torch.no_grad():
    for name, param in model.named_parameters():
        if param.grad is not None:
            grad_norm = param.grad.norm().item()
            if name not in grad_history:
                grad_history[name] = []
            grad_history[name].append(grad_norm)

param.grad at this point is the fully chain-ruled quantity from (E.13.17) — PyTorch's autograd engine walked the unrolled sequence backward and accumulated it for us. Watching grad_history per layer, per epoch, is a cheap way to see vanishing/exploding gradients happening in a VanillaRNN before they show up as a stalled or diverging loss curve. (This history is currently computed but not yet plotted anywhere — a natural next step.)

5. Where gating comes in

The book's fix for vanishing gradients is a gate: a sigmoid layer \(\gamma \in [0,1]\) that decides, per feature, whether to overwrite the state or keep it untouched:

\[ f(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \mathbf{x}_i) = \gamma(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \mathbf{x}_i) \odot \phi(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_i) + \big(1 - \gamma(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \mathbf{x}_i)\big) \odot \mathbf{s}_{i-1} \] Li-GRU gate

When \(\gamma \approx 1\), that timestep's factor in the BPTT product (E.13.16) is close to identity — the gradient passes through largely unscaled, which is precisely what prevents the vanishing behaviour above over long sequences. GRU adds a second ("reset") gate on top of this; LSTM adds a third ("forget") gate and a separate cell state. Both are exactly the models this project trains alongside the vanilla RNN:

src/models/gru.py 
self.gru = nn.GRU(input_size=input_dim, hidden_size=hidden_dim,
                   num_layers=num_layers, batch_first=True)
src/models/lstm.py 
self.lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=input_dim, hidden_size=hidden_dim,
                     num_layers=num_layers, batch_first=True)

Same \(f, g\) abstraction as the vanilla RNN, just with the internal gating machinery swapped in — which is also why exp1_gru.py, exp1_lstm.py, and exp1_rnn.py in this project share almost identical Optuna search code and only swap the model class.
